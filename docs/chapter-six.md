### §6.1: solving the inverse valuation equation

[DecimalConverseStep](../src/AoP/Chapter06/DecimalConverseStep.agda) starts with the specification `eval ds ≡ n` for positive `n`. Unrolling it gives two possibilities: a single nonzero digit, or a prefix representing `q` followed by a digit `d` with `n = 10q + d`. `roll` reads either witness back as a valuation equation. `Fixed.sound` and `Fixed.complete` show by induction on the candidate digits that any relation satisfying both directions describes exactly these numerals. This induction checks supplied digits. Computing them from `n` needs a branch decision and a decreasing quotient, supplied by the division and accumulator proofs below. [DecimalPrinterRefinement](../src/AoP/Chapter06/DecimalPrinterRefinement.agda) connects their output list to this specification. The printer’s nonzero first digit initializes a canonical numeral; parsing each further digit shifts the magnitude by one decimal place and adds the digit. `read-parse` proves agreement with the accumulator’s reader, and `positive` proves the resulting numeral represents the original positive input. `agrees` proves that the quotient algorithm and the Chapter 1 successor algorithm return the same canonical digits: reprint the valuation with the Chapter 1 inverse and apply `positive`. Thus `quotient-printer` satisfies both inverse laws, including recovery of every canonical input numeral. The separate singleton-zero convention applies only to the natural-number interface.

### §6.2: bounding a uniquely determined fold

[UniqueFixedBounds](../src/AoP/Chapter06/UniqueFixedBounds.agda) derives the order argument behind (6.2) and (6.3). Suppose the fold is the unique fixed point of its monotone step `Φ`, up to mutual inclusion. A proposed upper bound `x` only has to satisfy `Φ x ≤ x`: the infimum of all such candidates is fixed by [Knaster–Tarski](../src/AoP/Chapter06/FixedPoint.agda), so uniqueness puts the fold below `x`. For a lower bound, reverse the order and apply the same proof to the supremum of candidates satisfying `x ≤ Φ x`. The module takes those two bounds as data rather than assuming all predicates can be resized.

[AbstractFoldBounds](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AbstractFoldBounds.agda) applies these tests to the candidate `s ∘ r` for equations (6.4)–(6.5). Here `r` folds with algebra `R` and `t` folds with algebra `T`. Expanding one `T`-step on `s ∘ r` exposes `T ∘ F s`, followed on its input side by the recursive child `F r` and the constructor inverse. An upper comparison `T ∘ F s ⊑ s ∘ R` therefore makes the candidate prefixed once the `R` layer is folded back up; hence `t ⊑ s ∘ r`. The opposite comparison uses unfolding instead and gives `s ∘ r ⊑ t`. Each direction needs only its own local inequality.

### §6.3: why the hylomorphism is least

[HyloDivisionBound](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HyloDivisionBound.agda) checks Theorem 6.2’s calculation. A producer `u` builds intermediate trees and a fold `c` consumes them. `fixed` shows that expanding `c ∘ u` by one layer gives the same relation, using the producer and consumer equations. For leastness, start with any candidate `x` closed under one hylomorphism step. Divide `x` by `u`: the resulting `d` is the greatest relation satisfying `d ∘ u ⊑ x`. `rearrange` passes the producer through the exposed layer; cancellation under the monotone functor then shows `d` is closed under the consumer step. Fold induction yields `c ⊑ d`, hence `c ∘ u ⊑ x`. Only this particular division and the stated fold induction rule are required.

[HyloBranchUnion](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HyloBranchUnion.agda) specializes the result to a sum of two layers in concrete `Rel` (Corollary 6.1). The producer chooses a tag, the recursive step preserves it, and the consumer runs the corresponding branch. Reading the tag splits every composite path into a left or right path; injecting the witnesses reconstructs the composite. This equality holds at every candidate relation, so `Least.From.induction` transfers the leastness proof as well as the fixed-point equation.

### §6.4: multiplication count for repeated squaring

[HalvingPowerWork](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HalvingPowerWork.agda) counts one multiplication for squaring the shared recursive answer and one extra multiplication when the low bit is set. If `n < 2ᵏ`, then `half n < 2ᵏ⁻¹` for positive `k`, so `bound` gives at most `2k` multiplications for exponent `n`. The bound is tight: `size` and `tight` prove that the all-ones exponent `2ᵏ − 1` takes exactly `2k` multiplications. Removing its low bit leaves the next shorter all-ones exponent, so every step incurs both charges. For `13 = 1101₂`, the recurrence counts four squares and three extra multiplications. This count excludes computing the quotient and bit, and does not assign unit running time to multiplication of large operands.

### §6.5: a least solution can be partial

[PositiveLoopFixed](../src/AoP/Chapter06/PositiveLoopFixed.agda) derives the example from two total functions. The producer sends zero to the base branch and every positive input back to itself. The consumer returns zero in the base branch and otherwise returns the recursive answer unchanged. `expand` and `contract` identify their one-step graph composite with two rules: admit `(0,0)`, or repeat an existing edge at a positive input. The least solution therefore contains only `(0,0)` and has no output at `1`. Identity satisfies the same equation but includes `(1,1)`. This is why totality of the two layer functions alone cannot justify a total recursive program.

[AccessiblePlus](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessiblePlus.agda) proves that replacing predecessor steps by nonempty paths preserves and reflects accessibility at each input. `extend` uses induction on the first edge and the resulting child's accessibility for the remaining path. `restrict` treats each original edge as a path of length one. The nonempty condition matters: allowing an empty path adds a recursive call from every input to itself.

[AccessibleNoDescent](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleNoDescent.agda) connects accessibility to the relational well-foundedness test `X ⊆ X ∘ R ⇒ X ⊆ ⊥` in `Rel`. Given an `X` edge, the premise supplies another edge at a predecessor with the same output. Accessibility induction rules this out. For the reverse implication, take `X` to mark inaccessible inputs: if such an input had no inaccessible predecessor, all of its predecessors would be accessible and so would the input itself. `Classical.backward` uses an explicit double-negation elimination assumption for this argument; the forward proof is constructive.

[AccessibleContainerHylo](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleContainerHylo.agda) proves Theorem 6.3 for shape-and-position layers. A child is any input requested at any position of an allowed producer step. To compare a rival fixed point with the relation of successful derivations, unfold a rival edge once; accessibility at the input supplies induction hypotheses for every child of that particular layer. Reassembling those child derivations gives the required parent derivation. The other inclusion is induction on successful derivations. Neither direction assumes that the producer or consumer is deterministic. For totality, `Entire.run` additionally requires a producer witness at each input and a consumer witness for each layer of answers. Accessibility lets it recurse at every position, collecting both the answer and its derivation before applying the consumer. For Corollary 6.2, `Function.eval` uses this construction as a program. `graph-in` and `graph-out` prove its graph is exactly the solution when the producer layer is unique and the consumer gives equal outputs for pointwise-equal child answers. No global choice or function extensionality is assumed.

[AccessibleFoldPreimage](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleFoldPreimage.agda) checks Corollary 6.3 for containers. Given an accessible output `a`, surjectivity of the algebra supplies a layer that produces `a`. Recursively find a preimage tree for each value in that layer, keeping the fold derivation alongside it. The constructor joins these trees; the algebra witness and the child derivations certify that the joined tree folds to `a`. If every output is accessible, the fold is surjective.

[ContainerConverseFold](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ContainerConverseFold.agda) proves Theorem 6.4 for shape-and-position layers. A function `f` assigns each value a tree. Assume each allowed algebra step describes the root of that tree and its children. Then any fold derivation for `a` must have tree `f a`: `sound` assembles the child equalities using an explicit constructor congruence for pointwise-equal child families. Conversely, surjectivity supplies a layer for `a`. Its root equation identifies the subtrees, so `complete` recurses on the children of the given tree and constructs a fold derivation. This direction needs neither constructor congruence nor an accessibility test on `a`. The book also obtains accessibility as an intermediate conclusion. `decrease` sends an algebra child at position `i` to the immediate subtree at the same position in `f a`; `pull` transfers structural accessibility of trees back to the values. `accessible` thus supplies the input hypothesis of Corollary 6.3 without using surjectivity. [SurjectiveFoldEquality](../src/AoP/Chapter06/SurjectiveFoldEquality.agda) checks the last step in an abstract allegory. Given a fold `h` with `f ∘ h ⊑ id`, entireness of `f` gives `h ⊑ f˘`. For the reverse inclusion, insert a preimage using surjectivity of `h`; reversing `f ∘ h ⊑ id` then contracts the return path. Together the inclusions identify `h` with `f˘`.

### §6.6: deriving quicksort from a pivot split

Specify the output as an ordered permutation of the input. For a nonempty input, remove one occurrence of a pivot `p` and distribute the remaining occurrences into `l` and `r`, with each member of `l` at most `p` and each member of `r` at least `p`. Suppose recursively that `L` and `R` are ordered permutations of those two lists. Calculate the bag of the proposed answer (`⊎` adds multiplicities):

```text
bag (L ++ [p] ++ R)
= bag L ⊎ {p} ⊎ bag R       concatenation adds occurrence counts
= bag l ⊎ {p} ⊎ bag r       recursive permutation guarantees
= bag input                 the split assigns every occurrence exactly once
```

For order, the recursive guarantees handle comparisons inside `L` and `R`. Permutation transfers the split bounds to their elements; any `x` in `L` and `y` in `R` satisfy `x ≤ p ≤ y`. Thus concatenating the two recursive outputs around `p` meets both requirements. Removing the pivot makes each recursive list strictly shorter. The empty input returns `[]`.

[QuickSplitWork](../src/AoP/Chapter06/QuickSplitWork.agda) checks the last-pivot instance on `[3,1,2,2]`. The root pivot `2` leaves `[1,2]` and `[3]`; the next pivot `2` leaves `[1]` and `[]`. Reassembly gives `[1,2,2,3]`: the two equal occurrences both survive. The root costs three pivot comparisons, its left child costs one, and singleton nodes cost zero. See the [sorting guide](sorting-coverage.md) for the split refinement, recursive equation, proofs of order and permutation, and worst-case cost.

### §6.7: why an empty search frontier certifies closure

[FrontierRolling](../src/AoP/Chapter06/FrontierRolling.agda) derives the recursive update from (6.9) for vertex sets. `Grow P Q` starts in `Q` and extends paths only to destinations outside `P`. If such a path revisits `Q`, its earlier portion can be dropped: restart at that frontier vertex. After the last visit, its first edge supplies a fresh seed and the remaining edges avoid both `P` and `Q`. `forward` uses this split and `backward` restores the edge from the old frontier. Together they show that scanning the frontier preserves the answer, given decidable membership in `Q`. [RelationalFrontierRolling](../src/AoP/Chapter06/RelationalFrontierRolling.agda) proves the relational equations column by column. Starting with seed relation `S` yields `R* ∘ S`; an empty frontier yields `P`; otherwise the next frontier is `(R ∘ Q) − (P ∪ Q)`. Each successor witness retains the same starting column, so discoveries from different seeds cannot be mixed across columns.

[ClosureFrontierInvariant](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ClosureFrontierInvariant.agda) proves the stopping rule for forward reachability. Initially the frontier `Q` contains the seeds and the finished set `P` is empty. Each round moves `Q` into `P` and forms the next frontier from successors of `Q` outside `P ∪ Q`. The invariant says all seeds are accounted for, every discovered vertex has a path from a seed, and all successors of finished vertices have been discovered. `Round.step` preserves these claims given a decision for membership in `P ∪ Q`. Once `Q` is empty, path induction shows that every reachable vertex belongs to `P`, and the stored witnesses prove the converse. [FiniteFrontierClosure](../src/AoP/Chapter06/FiniteFrontierClosure.agda) turns the invariant into an executable reachability decision given decisions for seeds and edges and a finite list covering the reachable vertices. The list may include extra vertices: the input certificate says only that every path from a seed ends in the list, not which listed vertices are reachable. [FiniteFrontierBudget](../src/AoP/Chapter06/FiniteFrontierBudget.agda) supplies the termination argument: each nonempty round removes one frontier vertex from a list of unused tokens. Any reachable vertex still unfinished after the round was outside that frontier, so its token remains. When testing for a predecessor in the frontier, only listed vertices need be searched: every frontier member already has a path from a seed. Thus at most the length of the covering list many nonempty rounds can occur, even if the list contains duplicates. This bounds rounds, not the cost of evaluating predicate decisions. `Covered.solve` therefore also handles an infinite vertex type with a supplied finite reachable cover. `Graph.solve` takes a finite cover of the whole universe as a special case. [FrontierCycleTrace](../src/AoP/Chapter06/FrontierCycleTrace.agda) starts at `0` on the natural numbers, with edges `0 → 1 → 0` and cover `[0,1,2]`. The solver accepts `0` and `1`, rejects the extra candidate `2`, and also rejects `8`, which lies outside the cover. Filtering the cover by those decisions gives the list `[0,1]`. In general `listed` turns each entry in the filtered list into a path witness, and `found` locates every reachable endpoint in that list. Repeated entries in the cover can yield repeated entries in the enumeration.

The proof establishes preservation of numeric value for every input, including the empty word. The worked example converts `110101₂` to `65₈`, both denoting 53.

[SimpleHylo](../src/AoP/Chapter06/SimpleHylo.agda) checks the cons-list case of exercise 6.10. For a fixed input, two successful producer runs must emit the same list. A simple consumer then gives the same result on that list. Inputs with no finite run need no result. [ContainerHyloSimple](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ContainerHyloSimple.agda) handles well-founded container derivations. A simple producer fixes the shape and child inputs; induction identifies the child outputs positionwise, and a consumer respecting pointwise agreement yields equal final outputs. `factor` constructs an intermediate tree from a completed run, and `fuse` consumes a fold and unfold on the same tree. Thus `composite-simple` also covers two successful runs through different intermediate trees. Neither relation must be total. [HyloSimpleDivision](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HyloSimpleDivision.agda) gives the abstract proof without inspecting the intermediate datatype. Let `h` be the least fixed point from Theorem 6.2. To prove `h ∘ h˘ ⊑ id`, take the candidate `d = id / h˘`. Unfold the rival run `h˘` once. Producer simplicity contracts the two producer steps; the remaining child comparison contracts by functoriality, monotonicity, converse preservation and `d ∘ h˘ ⊑ id`. Consumer simplicity contracts the parent comparison. Thus `step d ⊑ d`, and leastness gives `h ⊑ d`, as required. This proof applies to any such relator with the indicated division and least fixed point; neither layer needs to be entire.

[HalvingRun](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HalvingRun.agda) checks the common termination and uniqueness argument for exercise 6.11. Every positive argument decreases when halved. The computed result is independent of the supplied bound, satisfies the bit-step recurrence, and agrees with every solution of those equations. [HalvingDigits](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HalvingDigits.agda) proves that doubling the quotient and adding the low bit reconstructs the input. Any interpretation respecting that digit step therefore equals the halving program. [HalvingPower](../src/AoP/Chapter06/HalvingPower.agda) instantiates that proof for exponentiation by squaring. Splitting the exponent into two equal halves gives the square; an odd low bit adds one multiplication by the base. [ModulusBound](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ModulusBound.agda) proves the corresponding range invariant for positive divisor `b`: shifting a remainder below `b` gives a value below `2b`, so at most one subtraction restores the bound. [ModulusStep](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ModulusStep.agda) implements the update and accounts for the shifted value exactly: it equals either the new remainder or the divisor plus that remainder. [BinaryModulus](../src/AoP/Chapter06/BinaryModulus.agda) lifts that certificate through the entire halving recursion: each bit doubles the number of old divisor copies, and a subtraction contributes one additional copy. For every natural input `n` and positive divisor `b`, the returned `r` is below `b` and adding copies of `b` to `r` gives `n`. The checked example gives `53 mod 5 = 3`.

[AccessibleFixed](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleFixed.agda) proves both directions of exercise 6.12 for predicates. A state satisfies `Box X` when all its predecessors satisfy `X`. Accessibility induction puts every accessible state in every fixed predicate. Conversely, both accessibility and the full predicate are fixed, so uniqueness up to mutual inclusion makes every state accessible.

[AccessibleTransfer](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleTransfer.agda) proves exercise 6.13. If every two-step `R` path can be replaced by an `S` step followed by an `R` step with the same endpoint, well-foundedness of `S` implies well-foundedness of `R`. The induction at `x` establishes accessibility of every `R`-child of `x`.

[AccessibleExtremes](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleExtremes.agda) checks exercise 6.14. Every point is accessible for the empty relation. A self-edge rules out accessibility at that point, which handles identity and the universal relation on inhabited carriers. Every relation on an empty carrier is vacuously well-founded.

[AccessibleMeet](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibleMeet.agda) checks exercise 6.15 for accessibility. Restricting a relation preserves accessibility, so a meet is well-founded if either factor is. On two vertices, each one-way edge alone is well-founded; their union admits an endless alternating path.

[AccessibilityMeasure](../src/AoP/Chapter06/AccessibilityMeasure.agda) proves exercise 6.16 for type-valued relations. Pulling a well-founded relation back along any function preserves accessibility: each recursive call descends to a predecessor of its measure. The measure may take the same value at distinct inputs.

[TreeMemberPath](../src/AoP/Chapter06/TreeMemberPath.agda) checks the two tree cases of exercise 6.17. Membership gives a path to a node carrying the sought label, and following such a path proves membership. A path ends at a tip in a tip-labelled tree; it may end at any fork in a fork-labelled tree. [ListMemberPath](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ListMemberPath.agda) checks the intermediate list equation `inlist = head ∘ tail*` in both directions: drop zero or more cells, then read the head of the resulting suffix. [MemberFold](../src/AoP/Chapter06/MemberFold.agda) derives list membership using the singleton and cons cases of the nonempty-list fold. For an inhabited element type, no ordinary relational cons-list fold directly returning an element can implement membership: a successful fold on a singleton needs a result on the empty tail, which has no members.

[ContainerEmpty](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ContainerEmpty.agda) checks a container case of exercise 6.21. If every value has a member, there are no values over the empty element type. Conversely, if each shape has decidably inhabited positions, emptiness over the empty element type implies that every value has a member. [ContainerEmptyConverse](../src/AoP/Chapter06/ContainerEmptyConverse.agda) identifies the logical strength of a converse uniform over all containers: it is equivalent to double-negation elimination for small types. For one shape with position type `P`, emptiness over the empty label type gives `¬¬P`; a member of the unit-labelled container supplies an actual position in `P`. [RelatorEmptyMembership](../src/AoP/Chapter06/RelatorEmptyMembership.agda) proves both directions for abstract relation liftings on concrete `Rel`, given the membership division law, composition preservation, and double-negation elimination for the converse. If a structure had no members, its membership column would fit the empty relation through `⊥`; expanding the composite column would produce an impossible intermediate value of `F ⊥`. A proof internal to arbitrary Boolean allegories remains open.

[MembershipDivisionLaw](../src/AoP/Chapter06/MembershipDivisionLaw.agda) proves 6.18 for concrete relations and a composition-preserving lifting. To extend the membership law from the identity to any relation `S`, expand the column `member ＼ S` using the identity law, combine the two lifted arrows, then contract the column at `R ∘ S`. `specialize` recovers the identity law by taking `S = Id`. [LargestMembership](../src/AoP/Chapter06/LargestMembership.agda) derives exercise 6.19 under the stated maximality hypothesis for identity. `natural` shows membership itself is lax. For any rival selector, `restrict` composes it with the identity column to obtain a lax transformation from identity to identity. Applying the hypothesis there and expanding the membership column proves that every selected value belongs to its input. [LargestRelatorTransformation](../src/AoP/Chapter06/LargestRelatorTransformation.agda) proves 6.20 for concrete relations under the membership laws of both relators. Its relation `Residual` sends a G-structure to every F-structure whose members all belong to the input. `natural` uses the composite-column law to prove laxity. For maximality, composing a competing transformation with F-membership gives a lax selector for G; 6.19 bounds it by G-membership. `largest-id` discharges the identity maximality hypothesis in `Rel` by testing with a singleton.
