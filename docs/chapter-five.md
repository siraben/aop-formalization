### §5.1: why a relator takes maps to maps

A map `f` in an allegory is entire and simple. Those conditions bound its two round trips: `id ⊑ f˘ ∘ f` and `f ∘ f˘ ⊑ id`. [RelatorMapConverse](../src/AoP/Chapter05/RelatorMapConverse.agda) transports the bounds through a monotone functor, using preservation of identity and composition. At this stage the transported right adjoint is `F(f˘)`. To identify it with `(F f)˘`, [AllegoryAdjointConverse](../src/AoP/Chapter05/AllegoryAdjointConverse.agda) proves Proposition 4.1 abstractly: apply the modular law to the unit to restrict the left arrow to its overlap with the converse of the right arrow; the counit shows the restriction still covers the whole left arrow. Reversing the adjunction gives the other inclusion. Consequently `F f` is a map and the two ways to form its converse agree (Lemma 5.1). Neither proof needs a tabulation.

[RelatorSpanConverse](../src/AoP/Chapter05/RelatorSpanConverse.agda) proves the forward direction of Theorem 5.1. Write `R = f ∘ g˘` using the maps from the rows of a table to the output and input. Reversing the relation swaps those maps, so `F(R˘)` reduces to `F g ∘ (F f)˘`. Reversing `F R` gives the same composite by the lemma and double converse. `Row.preserve` requires just the factorization equation and map legs; uniqueness of rows plays no role in this direction.

[RelatorLegAgreement](../src/AoP/Chapter05/RelatorLegAgreement.agda) proves Corollary 5.1: two relators with the same object action that agree on maps agree on every tabulated arrow. Expand each image of `R` using the same two legs `f` and `g`. Agreement on `f` identifies the first factors, and agreement on `g` identifies their converses in the second factors. `Agreement.agree` then folds the second composite back to `G R`. The conclusion compares arrow values using the target category’s equivalence.

**Reverse direction: the mediator step needs a hypothesis.** The calculation contracts `F m ∘ (F m)˘` to an inclusion below identity, using simplicity of the image of the row map `m`. [ConverseFunctorWitness](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ConverseFunctorWitness.agda) tests this step on the finite category of decidable relations between the empty set and a singleton. [TwoObjectAllegory](../src/AoP/Chapter05/TwoObjectAllegory.agda) checks the source allegory laws and supplies a jointly monic table for each arrow. Empty arrows factor through zero rows and the single nonempty arrow through one row. The functor sends the two objects to a singleton and a Boolean pair; it sends empty arrows to full rectangles and the singleton identity to Boolean equality. This preserves identities, composition and converse. But the source empty relation is included in the singleton identity, whereas the full Boolean rectangle contains `(false,true)` and equality does not. In particular, the lifted empty-to-singleton row map has both outputs at its sole input. Converse preservation alone cannot supply that simplicity inequality here. [ConverseMediatorOrder](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ConverseMediatorOrder.agda) proves the conditional calculation. The smaller span has legs `f ∘ m` and `g ∘ m`; expanding its image exposes `F m ∘ (F m)˘` in the middle. If this lies below identity, the remaining factors form precisely the image of the larger span. The bound is needed only for the mediator in this comparison.

### §5.2: projecting a relational pair

For functions, projecting the first component of a pair recovers the first function. What changes when either component can have no output? Let `R` and `S` be the two branches and fix input `a` and proposed left output `b`. Expand the path to the projection:

```text
(fst ∘ pair R S) b a
⇔ ∃ c. R b a ∧ S c a        the intermediate pair must be (b,c)
⇔ R b a ∧ (∃ c. S c a)      the left edge does not depend on c
⇔ (R ∘ dom S) b a           dom S tests a and leaves it unchanged
```

For example, if `R` returns `7` at `a` but `S` has no output there, the pair has no output to project. The last line predicts this: `a` fails the domain test before `R` can run.

[PairProjectionDomains](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PairProjectionDomains.agda) proves (5.6)–(5.7) in `Rel`. To project the left output of `(R,S)`, both branches must first produce values. The discarded right value is exactly the witness for `dom S`, giving `fst ∘ pair R S = R ∘ dom S`. The reverse proof takes that witness and reconstructs the intermediate pair. Swapping branches gives the right projection law. `necessary` and `unrestricted` identify exactly when the left projection equals `R`: the right branch needs an output at every input used by `R`.

[ModularPairPull](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ModularPairPull.agda) checks the abstract modular calculation for (5.4), given the two stated projection laws for `t = r × id`. The first law rewrites the changed left branch as `t` after the old left branch. Modularity then pulls `t` outside the intersection, leaving a right branch restricted by `t˘`. The second projection law bounds that branch by the original right branch, completing the inclusion. The hypotheses allow the product object to change when the type of the left output changes. [PairPullInstance](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PairPullInstance.agda) discharges both hypotheses for relations on sets: the unchanged right coordinate supplies the intermediate value, and its identity edge guarantees the right projection is preserved. It uses [ConcreteAllegory](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ConcreteAllegory.agda) to interpret the abstract proof with relation witnesses.

[PairAbsorptionCalculation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PairAbsorptionCalculation.agda) combines the two one-sided rules to derive the reverse inclusion of (5.3). First undo the left coordinate change, then undo the right one. The intermediate pairs are generally different: the right coordinate has already changed in the first pair and still has its old value in the second. `combine` eliminates the unchanged-coordinate identity edges to obtain one simultaneous step. The derivation does not use preservation of tensor composition. [TensorCompositionByAbsorption](../src/AoP/Chapter05/TensorCompositionByAbsorption.agda) uses the result to derive that law in `Rel`. Expand the inner tensor as a pairing after the two projections, apply absorption, then evaluate each projection. Going backwards, `expose` restores the projection edges, `absorb` supplies the intermediate pair, and `pack` recovers the inner tensor. [TensorBifunctor](../src/AoP/Chapter05/TensorBifunctor.agda) packages the calculation as a functor from `Rel × Rel` to `Rel`. Its identity law pairs the two identity edges; monotonicity transports the left and right witnesses independently.

[PairCancellationCalculation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PairCancellationCalculation.agda) derives (5.8) in `Rel`. To follow a pairing by the converse of another pairing, run the two converse branches independently and then test their chosen sources with the diagonal `a ↦ (a,a)`. Absorption joins each branch to its own preceding path. The diagonal requires the paths to end at the same `a`, which gives exactly their intersection. `factor`, `absorb`, and `cancel` perform these steps; `restore`, `expand`, and `unfactor` reconstruct the shared pair witness.

### §5.3: case analysis for relations

[PowerCopair](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PowerCopair.agda) constructs a relation out of a sum using ordinary case analysis. Transpose the two branch relations so each input selects its fibre of possible outputs. Ordinary case analysis chooses the appropriate fibre, and membership reads an output from it. `first` and `second` check this relation on each injection. For uniqueness, transpose a competing relation: `Rival.branch` gives its two ordinary case equations, so ordinary uniqueness identifies its fibre function, and transpose cancellation recovers the relation. The proof needs a representing power object only at the chosen output and the ordinary coproduct equations at that fibre type. [SumCopairCalculation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/SumCopairCalculation.agda) derives (5.10) in `Rel`: each branch runs its own relation and tags the output. `tagged` reads the injection edge to recover a sum-relation witness; `expand` supplies that edge to reconstruct the branch composite.

### §5.4: extending direct image to relations

[PowerGraphExtension](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PowerGraphExtension.agda) proves that the two coverage conditions in [PowerRelator](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PowerRelator.agda) give exactly direct image on a function graph. Suppose `Y` is related to `X` by the lift of `f`. Backward coverage gives a preimage in `X` for each element of `Y`. Forward coverage supplies a member of `Y` related to each `a` in `X`; the graph equation forces that member to be `f a`. These are the two inclusions `Y ≐ ℰ (graph f) X`. `rebuild` recovers both coverage witnesses from them. For arbitrary relations, `monotone` reuses the same witnesses when the relation grows.

For composition, take the middle points reached from `X` by `R` that can also reach `Z` by `S`. [PowerMiddleBounds](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PowerMiddleBounds.agda) identifies this set as the intersection of two direct images. `largest` proves every possible intermediate set is contained in it: at each admitted point, the two coverage proofs supply its incoming and outgoing edges. `enlarge` proves the whole intersection still covers both endpoints whenever any factorization does. This explains why the composition proof needs no choice of a preferred path.

### §5.5: folding a relation by collecting its answers

How can an ordinary list fold implement a specification that allows several answers? Give the fold a set of answers as its state. Write `Run b xs` for a derivation of output `b` on `xs` and `answers xs = {b | Run b xs}`. Let `base` be the allowed outputs on `[]`, and let `step a t b` mean that the head `a` can turn tail result `t` into `b`. Calculate membership at a cons cell:

```text
b ∈ answers (a ∷ xs)
⇔ Run b (a ∷ xs)                          definition of answers
⇔ ∃ t. Run t xs ∧ step a t b              unpack the last rule of the run
⇔ ∃ t. t ∈ answers xs ∧ step a t b        definition on the tail
⇔ b ∈ advance a (answers xs)              collect all successors of tail answers
```

At `[]` the corresponding test is just `b ∈ base`. Together these equations give `answers = foldr advance base`, with sets compared by their members. An empty tail-result set contributes no successors; two possible tail results both contribute theirs. The calculation never needs to choose a single run.

[RelationalFoldTransfer](../src/AoP/Chapter05/RelationalFoldTransfer.agda) checks the abstract calculation in (5.12). Given a relational algebra `r`, lift membership through one datatype layer and transpose `r ∘ H mem` to form an ordinary algebra `alg` on the power object. Fold `alg`, then read its result by membership. The key equation `layer` shows that reading one ordinary step equals taking one relational step. `solves` uses this equation forwards; `Rival.only` transposes any other solution and uses it backwards to obtain the ordinary fold equation. Ordinary fold uniqueness gives the Eilenberg–Wright lemma, `transpose-fold`: transposing any solution yields the ordinary fold on fibres. Reading the fibres back by membership then identifies the relations. The hypotheses specify the graph action, composition and congruence of the lifting, the power transpose at the output, and the ordinary fold’s computation and uniqueness laws.

[ListFoldByMembership](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ListFoldByMembership.agda) derives (5.12) for cons-lists. The empty case returns the predicate of allowed base answers. At a cons cell, an output `b` is admitted if a tail answer `t` belongs to the recursively computed predicate and the algebra can take `(a,t)` to `b`. Reading membership in the resulting predicate gives the relational fold. `sound` and `complete` convert between membership witnesses and fold derivations. `Rival.only` shows that any relation satisfying the two algebra equations has exactly those outputs at each list; the induction transports the tail witness without choosing a unique tail answer.

[AutomatonFibreFold](../src/AoP/Chapter05/AutomatonFibreFold.agda) illustrates the Eilenberg–Wright lemma with an executable three-bit state. On `guess`, an idle machine can stay idle or arm itself. Only an armed machine can take `finish`. The list fold processes the tail first: for `[finish, guess]`, the reachable states are `{idle} → {idle, armed} → {accepted}`. The deterministic update copies the idle bit to both possible `guess` outputs and copies the armed bit to the `finish` output. `correct` proves for every list and every state that its bit is set exactly when a relational run reaches it.

[TypeLiftConverse](../src/AoP/Chapter05/TypeLiftConverse.agda) checks the type-lifting calculation at the end of §5.5. Start with the fold equation in the form `t = b ∘ layer(t,r) ∘ a˘`. Taking converse swaps the constructors; preservation of converse by the layer rewrites the middle term to `back(t˘,r˘)`. Thus `t˘` satisfies the equation defining the lift of `r˘`, and fold uniqueness identifies them. `from-fold` starts from ordinary fold computation `t ∘ a ≈ b ∘ layer(t,r)` and ends with ordinary fold uniqueness. To move the source constructor to the other side, insert `a ∘ a˘ ≈ id`; after reversing, remove `b˘ ∘ b ≈ id` to put the equation back in fold form. These two constructor inverse laws suffice for converse preservation. The monotonicity issue in §5.1 is separate.

### §5.6: deriving subsequence enumeration

[SubsequenceLayerCalculation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/SubsequenceLayerCalculation.agda) calculates the update for [subsequence enumeration](../src/AoP/Chapter05/Subsequences.agda). The empty input has one answer, the empty list. Given a head `a` and a predicate of tail answers, `keep-or-skip` splits the next answers into the image under `a ∷_` and the unchanged tail answers. If `pool` enumerates the tail answers, `update` identifies membership in this union with membership in `map (a ∷_) pool ++ pool`. This works for any accumulator and allows duplicate witnesses. For input `[a,a]`, keeping only the first or only the second occurrence produces the same singleton `[a]`.

[CartesianMenuPairs](../src/AoP/Chapter05/CartesianMenuPairs.agda) calculates the two-list product `cpp`. For each left value `a`, generate one row pairing `a` with every right value, then concatenate the rows. `pairs` proves that `(a,b)` occurs exactly when `a` occurs in the left menu and `b` in the right. Reading the proof backwards, the left membership witness selects a row and the right witness selects a position within it. `column` checks the companion `cpl` calculation when the right value is fixed. Neither calculation needs decidable equality or removal of duplicates.

[ChoiceProductFold](../src/AoP/Chapter05/ChoiceProductFold.agda) derives `cplist` from this two-menu product. Start with one empty selection. For each menu, `cpp` pairs each possible head with each valid selection from the remaining menus; `map cons` turns those pairs into lists. `sound` and `complete` prove agreement with the one-entry-per-menu specification `Pick`. For `[[1,2],[3,4]]`, the suffix selections are `[[3],[4]]` and the final selections are `[[1,3],[1,4],[2,3],[2,4]]`.

[PrefixFoldCuts](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PrefixFoldCuts.agda) relates three prefix specifications. The fold either stops at a cons cell or keeps its head and recurses. `to-cut` and `from-cut` identify its answers with lists `p` for which `p ++ tail ≡ xs`. Repeatedly deleting the last element gives the same answers: `remove` constructs a path that deletes precisely the suffix, and `recover` reads a suffix back from any such path. The zero-step path accounts for the whole input as a prefix. [PrefixFoldEnumeration](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PrefixFoldEnumeration.agda) proves that `inits` enumerates exactly the fold answers. Stopping emits one empty prefix even if the tail has many prefixes: the justification needs just one tail answer, and the empty prefix always supplies it. Retaining the head maps cons over the recursive enumeration.

[SuffixPathCuts](../src/AoP/Chapter05/SuffixPathCuts.agda) checks the dual calculation for `tails`. A path of tail steps records precisely the discarded prefix: `cut` reads it in order, and `remove` rebuilds the path. Each step skips one entry in the suffix enumeration, so `enumerate` and `justify` identify paths with membership in `tails xs`. The zero-step path selects the entire input, including when that input is empty.

[PartitionLayerInverse](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionLayerInverse.agda) proves that the `new`/`glue` fold describes exactly the partitions whose blocks flatten to the input. `peel` recovers the last construction step by inspecting the first block: a singleton came from `new`; a longer block came from `glue`. `commute` shows that either step prepends one element after flattening. This recovers the head and the recursive tail equation needed by `complete`. Empty input has the single partition with no blocks. [PartitionPoolFold](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionPoolFold.agda) derives the executable update: for each tail partition emit one row containing the `new` result and, when the tail has a first block, the `glue` result. Concatenating the rows enumerates all next partitions. `read` and `write` relate row membership to a single algebra step; `sound` and `complete-pool` lift that equivalence to whole lists.

[BagFoldExchange](../src/AoP/Chapter05/BagFoldExchange.agda) characterizes which list folds ignore ordering. If two additions can exchange places at every state produced by the fold, `gap` moves a new element past one head at a time, and `invariant` shows that any permutation preserves the result. Conversely, permuting just the first two entries forces the exchange law at every reachable state. `everywhere` extends the law to all states when each bag has a list representative. This property ensures order independence; by itself it does not guarantee that the fold distinguishes multiplicities.

### §5.7: why the lax square becomes equality on maps

[LaxMapSquare](../src/AoP/Chapter05/LaxMapSquare.agda) checks the shunting step of Theorem 5.2. Suppose the two images of a map are `f` and `g`, and the transformation components at its endpoints are `p` and `q`. Laxity gives `q ∘ f ⊑ g ∘ p`. To obtain the opposite inclusion, apply laxity to the converse arrow and use preservation of converse to obtain `p ∘ f˘ ⊑ g˘ ∘ q`. The map units and counits move `f˘` and `g˘` across this inclusion; `exact` combines the two bounds. The components themselves need not be maps. For the other direction, [LaxSquareComposition](../src/AoP/Chapter05/LaxSquareComposition.agda) proves that the squares for two consecutive arrows compose: reassociate, apply the first bound, reassociate at the intermediate component, then apply the second bound. [LaxTabulationTheorem](../src/AoP/Chapter05/LaxTabulationTheorem.agda) assembles both directions for two monotone functors. `Leg.exact` applies laxity to a map and its converse. Conversely, write a relation as `r = f ∘ g˘`. `Row.extend` expands both functor images on this table, uses ordinary naturality on `f`, shunts ordinary naturality on `g` to the converse square, and pastes them. Thus naturality on maps suffices for laxity on every relation supplied with such a factorization.

Pair the prefixes in increasing length with these suffixes in decreasing length. The resulting list agrees in order and membership with the direct split enumeration, including both endpoint cuts.

[InitFold](../src/AoP/Chapter05/InitFold.agda) checks exercise 5.24: folding a nonempty list with an empty singleton result and a cons step removes exactly its last element. The proof reconstructs the input by appending the discarded element and shows that any such decomposition yields the same prefix.

[PartitionRanges](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionRanges.agda) checks both range identities of exercise 5.25. Starting a new block produces precisely a singleton first block. Gluing produces precisely a first block with at least two elements. In either case all later blocks stay unchanged.

[PartitionFlatten](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionFlatten.agda) checks exercise 5.26: the enumeration returns a list of nonempty blocks exactly when flattening those blocks gives the original input. The empty input has one partition, the empty list of blocks. [PartitionThree](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionThree.agda) checks the book’s full example: the two gaps in `[1,2,3]` yield exactly four partitions, by cutting both, only the first, only the last, or neither. For a nonempty candidate the completeness proof removes either its first singleton block or the first element of a longer block, reconstructing the `new` or `glue` step.

[PartitionPredicate](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PartitionPredicate.agda) checks exercise 5.27: fold directly into a predicate on block lists. Each step takes the union of the `new` and `glue` images. Both directions of the proof identify that predicate with membership in the enumeration from exercise 5.26.

[PermuteTail](../src/AoP/Chapter05/PermuteTail.agda) checks the identity in exercise 5.28 for the insertion-based permutation relation: permuting the tail first leaves the set of final permutations unchanged. [BagTailCalculation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/BagTailCalculation.agda) gives the bag-based derivation assuming every bag has a representative list. It uses `bagify ∘ bagify˘ = id` to insert a tail representative, then the cons-fold equation to remove that intermediate list. [BagCountPermutation](../src/AoP/Chapter05/BagCountPermutation.agda) identifies the concrete insertion relation with equality of multiplicity functions for element types with decidable equality. In the reverse direction a positive count locates an occurrence; removing it cancels one increment on both sides and reduces the source list. The abstract bag laws alone do not assert this multiplicity characterization.

[InterleaveFold](../src/AoP/Chapter05/InterleaveFold.agda) checks exercise 5.29. A fold assigns each output entry to one of two lists while preserving order in each. Its converse agrees in both directions with consuming the next head of either input. The book’s example interleaves `[1,2,3,4]` and `[10,11,12]` to obtain `[1,10,2,3,11,12,4]`.

[LaxChoices](../src/AoP/Chapter05/LaxChoices.agda) checks exercise 5.30: wrapping one chosen output gives a cover of singletons; duplicating one output gives two compatible choices; taking the union of two covers gives a cover of the union. The unnumbered examples and intermediate calculations still need a full audit.
